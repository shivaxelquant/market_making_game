    How to play

    You and the bots are trading one contract: the sum of every card in play. That is each player's two private cards plus the three centre cards. Nobody sees the whole table until settlement, so every price is a guess about cards you cannot see.

    Card values

    Number cards count ten times their rank, in any suit. Face cards and aces are worth a lot, but their sign depends on colour. Red ones add to the total and black ones subtract from it.

    The deal

    1. Each player gets two private cards. You see yours face up; the bots' cards stay face down.
    2. Three centre cards go face down in the middle. They belong to nobody, but they count toward the total.
    3. The rest of the 52-card deck is out of play.

    A round

    1. Each round, every player makes one two-way market in turn, a bid and an ask such as 530 @ 550. The starting seat rotates each round.
    2. The ask must be above the bid, and the spread (ask minus bid) cannot exceed the max spread setting.
    3. When a market goes up, every other player answers at the same time: buy at the ask, sell at the bid, or pass. Each answer can be 1 lot up to the Max lots setting (5 by default). The cap is the same for you and every bot, and each answer trades against the player who quoted.
    4. After rounds 1, 2 and 3, one centre card is turned face up. Round 4 trades with all three centre cards showing.
    5. After round 4, every hand is revealed and all trades settle against the true total.

    Positions and settlement

    Buying at p earns total − p per lot; selling at p earns p − total. With a position limit on, nobody may be longer or shorter than the limit. An order that would break the limit, for either the quoter or the responder, is cut down to what fits, or cancelled if nothing fits. Fills go in seat order after the quoter, so a large early order can use up the quoter's room.

    The shot clock

    The clock is off by default. Switch it on to give yourself a fixed number of seconds per decision. If time runs out while you are quoting, your market is skipped for that turn. If it runs out while you are answering a bot's market, you pass. Your decision times are recorded either way, so you can compare how you play with and without the clock in Your results below.

    Controls

    • Quoting: type a bid and the ask fills in at the max spread. Change either one, then press Enter.
    • Answering: press 1–5 to pick how many lots, then B buy at the ask, S sell at the bid, or P pass. Your last size carries over to the next answer.
    • Coach shows your own-cards fair value and what each bot's market implies about its hand. Games played with Coach on are marked in your results.

    How the bots think

    The bots are risk-neutral Bayesian traders. Every number they use is computed exactly from the cards still unseen. They do not cheat: a bot sees only its own two cards, the centre cards that are face up, and the public tape. The Bot policy setting picks how they play:

    • Exact: every bot quotes its true fair value every time. This is easy to read and, as shown below, beatable.
    • Mixed (default): each deal, every bot secretly chooses to quote openly or to guard its hand. The bots also learn your style from your past hands.
    • Fallible: Mixed, plus one bot chosen at random makes errors when it quotes and answers.

    1 · Prior over a hand

    Every unseen card is equally likely to be anywhere. A bot lists every pair of unseen cards, about 1,100 pairs, to get the exact distribution of another player's two-card sum, on a 10-point grid from −280 to +280.

    2 · Fair value

    FV = own hand + centre shown + Σ E[each other hand] + hidden centre × avg(left) avg(left) = (unseen points − Σ E[hands]) ÷ (unseen cards − cards in hands)

    The last term handles card removal. If the other hands look rich in points, the centre cards that remain are expected to be poorer.

    3 · Reading a market

    A quote is centred on the quoter's fair value. Given everything public, that fair value is a straight line in the quoter's hidden hand sum s:

    mid ≈ A + B·s B = (1 − β) × (1 − hidden centre ÷ cards left)

    A collects the public information. β is the card-removal effect of a strong hand on everyone else. Because B is known, the midpoint can be inverted into a hand sum. Bots score every possible s with a Gaussian likelihood and update by Bayes' rule:

    P(s | quotes) ∝ P(s) × Π N(mid; A + B·s, σ)

    Against another bot, σ = 4 points, with a 2% chance that any single signal is noise. After one full round, the bots know each other's hand sums to within a few points.

    4 · Reading you

    Bots do not assume you play like them. In Exact play they keep two models of you: you quote around your full-information value, or around the simple value of your own cards. Your signals get σ = 25 points and a 30% chance of being noise. Your buys and sells count as evidence; your passes do not.

    In Mixed and Fallible play they keep five models of you: full-information or own-cards, each tight (σ = 15) or loose (σ = 40), plus guarded, where your markets carry no information but your answers do. Within a deal, each model is weighted by how well it explains everything you have done. After each deal your hand is face up, so the bots score each model against what you actually held and carry those odds into the next deal, with older deals fading by 10% a game. Deliberate passes count as evidence here, because a pass says your value sits inside the market. Timeouts never count. In every policy, bots read the direction of your trades but not their size, because people choose size for many reasons.

    5 · Answering a market

    A bot first updates on the market it was shown, because the quote itself is information. Then it compares the market with its reservation price r = FV − κ·position:

    buy if r − ask ≥ 1 sell if bid − r ≥ 1 otherwise pass

    With a position limit on, κ = max spread ÷ (2 × limit). This lowers a long bot's bid and raises a short bot's offer, so it keeps room to trade later. With no limit, κ = 0 and the bot simply maximises expected profit.

    Size. Bots have the same lot cap as you. A risk-neutral bot keeps adding lots while each extra lot still clears the bar: lot n needs r − κ·(n − 1) − ask ≥ 1. With no position limit κ = 0, so any edge means the full size. With a limit on, a bot's size shows roughly how big its edge is, and the other bots read it that way: n lots means lot n cleared the bar and lot n + 1 did not.

    6 · Making a market

    A bot centres its market on FV − κ·position and quotes the full allowed spread. Only one market is live at a time and no rival maker can undercut it, so a wider spread gives up less to better-informed players. Answers count as evidence too: a pass tells the table that the bot's value sits inside the market.

    7 · Mixed policy

    At each deal, every bot secretly draws a style: open (60%) or guarded (40%). An open bot quotes its fair value. A guarded bot quotes the value the table already expects from it, which is A + B × (public estimate of its hand), so its market adds nothing to what everyone already knew. Both styles answer markets the same way, by their true fair value. Everyone knows the 60/40 odds; nobody knows which bot drew which.

    The odds of each style are judged from public evidence only, so every seat agrees on them. Without this, bots holding different cards would disagree about a player's style, and the shared reading model in section 3 would break.

    8 · Fallible policy

    Same as Mixed, but one bot chosen at random each deal is erratic: its quotes and its answers carry random errors of about ±25 points. The other bots know that one of them is erratic, a 1-in-N chance each, but not which. At settlement, every bot's style is shown under its seat.

    What this means for you: against Exact bots, a market tells you the hand. Against Mixed bots, only open bots' markets do, and you cannot be sure which those are. Either way, your own market tells them about yours. The opener in round 1 is the most exposed seat, because everyone who answers knows the opener's information plus their own.

    9 · Handicaps

    Two settings make the bots stronger by giving them information they would not have in a fair game. Both are off by default, and both can be combined with any policy.

    • Secret bot team (2 or 3 bots): the team's bots see each other's hands and never trade with each other. When a team bot makes a market, it quotes as if it knew only its own hand, so its market reads like anyone else's and gives the team away to nobody. When it answers other markets, it uses every team card. Nobody is told who is on the team until settlement.
    • Bots see your cards (one or both): every bot sees that card's value. The card leaves the unseen deck in their maths, and with one card seen they only need to estimate the other. Every bot knows the others can see it too, and so do you, so the reading model allows for it whenever anyone reads a bot's market. With both cards seen, the bots know everything you know plus their own hands.

    With both cards seen, you have no private information left, so by the same zero-sum argument below your best possible average is below zero. You can only gain from the bots' own mistakes.

    This test plays a batch of games with a scripted player in your seat, against the bot policy and settings selected at the top. The script never gets tired or rushed, so the result shows what perfect execution of its strategy earns. The bots' memory of the script's style starts empty and builds up over the batch, just as it does for you.

    The scripted players

    • Concealer quotes the value the table already expects from it, so its markets reveal nothing about its hand. It answers every market using everything it knows, including every hand it has read, and trades the full allowed size whenever it has an edge.
    • Honest quotes and answers at its full-information fair value, the same numbers a bot in your seat would use. It also trades full size whenever it has an edge.

    Why Exact bots can be beaten

    1. An Exact bot's market midpoint is a fixed function of its hand, A + B·s. Anyone can invert it and read the hand to within about a point.
    2. The Concealer's market equals what the table already expects, so the bots learn nothing from it. They learn about its hand only from its trades, and Exact bots discount those.
    3. After one round, the Concealer knows every hand. Each bot knows every hand except the Concealer's. From the Concealer's side, every later bot market is mispriced by about 0.92 × D, where D is the Concealer's hand minus the bots' estimate of it.
    4. The Concealer trades only when that gap beats the half-spread, so its expected gain per bot market is E[(|0.92·D| − spread/2)⁺]. That is above zero whenever D is uncertain. Most of it is collected while answering in round 1, until the position limit stops it.
    5. Even the Honest script gains a little against Exact bots. They read the human seat loosely (σ = 25, 30% noise), while the human reads them almost exactly (σ = 4).

    Why random noise does not help

    Suppose a bot quotes its fair value plus a random error η. A responder whose information is better by X takes E[(|X − η| − h)⁺], where h is the half-spread. The function (|x| − h)⁺ is convex, so by Jensen's inequality the noise can only raise that amount above E[(|X| − h)⁺]. The noise was meant to hide the bot's hand, but the bot's answers still reveal it, so the bot pays the cost and gets little cover.

    What works is randomising which information a quote uses, not the price. A guarded quote is exactly what the table already expects, so its gap from the bot's own fair value comes entirely from the bot's private cards. Nobody else can see that gap, so nobody can trade against it. That is the Mixed policy.

    Why no strategy is guaranteed an edge

    The game is zero-sum, and with the opening seat drawn at random, every seat is identical. Nash's theorem says such a game has a symmetric equilibrium, and in a symmetric zero-sum equilibrium every seat averages exactly 0. By definition of an equilibrium, no single player gains by changing strategy alone. So against bots playing an equilibrium, the best a perfect player can average is 0.

    Your edge comes only from the bots' mistakes. Exact and Fallible bots make mistakes you can measure. Mixed bots are not a proven equilibrium, and some cleverer strategy may still beat them, but none of the three scripts found an edge.

    How many games you need

    One game's P&L has a standard deviation of about 450 points with 1-lot answers, and about 900 with up to 5 lots. An edge of E points per game takes about (1.96 × SD / E)² games to show at 95% confidence. With 1 lot, that is about 80 games for a 100-point edge. With 5 lots, it is about 310 games for a 100-point edge and about 1,250 for a 50-point edge. A good player will still lose plenty of single games, and larger size makes the swings bigger.
